01 / Define the system

One repeatable cooling module. Two separate liquid circuits.

The reference design transfers 1 MW into liquid across ten parallel racks. Each rack has an assumed 100 kW liquid load; residual air-cooled heat is a separate requirement. These are independent study assumptions, not AWS or Google operating specifications.

Facility-water system · FWS

Heat-rejection plant → CDU heat exchanger → heat-rejection plant. Climate, water availability, heat reuse and approach temperatures determine the dry-cooler, economizer or chiller configuration.

Technology-cooling system · TCS

CDU exchanger → pump bank → supply header → rack manifolds and parallel server paths → return header → exchanger. The exchanger transfers heat while the two coolant streams remain separate.

A liquid-to-air sidecar is a future topology comparison. It transfers heat to the room air system; it does not eliminate the building's heat-rejection duty. “Modular” here means a repeatable cooling block, rather than a claim about a particular containerized facility.

02 / Learn from public hardware

Published assemblies, with their evidence boundaries.

What these references establish—and what remains unknown

AWS publishes a direct-to-chip approach and its In-Row Heat Exchanger (IRHX), while Google documents both liquid-to-liquid Deschutes and liquid-to-air Brazos architectures. We are not asserting that every AWS or Google site uses one universal cooling platform.

Exact AWS pump curves, coolant formulation, piping schedules and as-built P&IDs were not available in the reviewed material. The OCP Deschutes contribution/specification was located, but its full PDF download was blocked during this review; specification-specific requirements remain unverified here.

Amazon patent US10201116B1 describes an older rack air-to-fluid cooling concept. It is design background, not proof that a current installation implements that patent. Third-party photographs remain hosted by their original publishers, retain attribution, and are excluded from our downloadable source bundle.

Equations, assumptions and what the calculator omits

Flow: ṁ = Q̇/(cp ΔT); q = ṁ/ρ; v = 4q/(πD²).

Loss: Δp = [fL/D + ΣK] ρv²/2. The calculation uses the Darcy friction factor: 64/Re in laminar flow and the Haaland approximation in turbulent flow. Transitional flow requires further treatment.

Pump: H = Δp/(ρg); Pelectric = qΔp/η. Parallel pumps share flow, not head. The critical path includes common piping and one rack's circulation path; parallel-rack losses are not added in series.

Missing at this stage: segmented header flow balance, actual pump curves, flow-dependent component maps, temperature-dependent fluid properties, unequal rack resistance, control dynamics, pump failure, thermal inventory, water hammer and physical calibration. High velocities are flagged as a screening concern, not as a universal code limit.

The dynamic pressure estimate does not establish a component pressure rating. A rating must account for fill pressure, pump shutoff, elevation, thermal expansion and transients.

04 / Turn estimates into a design

Choose the route and components together.

Pipe size, length and bends

Compare 125/40 mm header/branch clear IDs against 150/50 mm. Actual DN/NPS size depends on wall thickness, ports and fittings. Route lengths come from dimensioned geometry; the current 40/20 m values are placeholders.

Use accessible service zones, compatible materials, standard long-radius elbows where practical, pipe supports, drains, high-point vents and hose bend-radius control. Compare direct return and reverse return; neither layout removes the need to balance rack flows.

Elevation and pump suction

In a filled closed loop, upward and downward hydrostatic heads cancel around the circuit for equal density. Five metres of elevation does not automatically add five metres of circulating pump head.

It still changes local pressure by approximately 49 kPa. Check highest-point pressure, lowest-point rating, fill/expansion pressure, air removal, and available NPSH using absolute suction pressure and coolant vapor pressure. Open-reservoir transfer adds net static head.

Pressure loss alone cannot select the exchanger. For an illustrative 25/35 °C facility stream and 35/45 °C technology stream, a 1 MW counterflow duty implies approximately 100 kW/K UA before fouling and pump heat. A dry cooler cannot deliver 25 °C water in 35 °C outdoor air; site climate may require warmer coolant or mechanical cooling.

Coolant chemistry, wetted metals, seals and water-quality limits must follow the selected equipment. The water-property assumption is not a chemistry specification. Check the outdoor facility loop separately for freeze protection.

05 / Spend CFD effort where it changes a decision

Solve the network in 1D. Resolve uncertain geometry in 3D.

Initial modeling method and escalation criteria
ComponentInitial methodCFD becomes useful when…
Straight pipe / standard elbowDarcy–Weisbach and tested fitting KCross-section is unusual or closely spaced bends create interacting separation.
Tees and distribution manifoldRun/branch correlations dependent on flow splitCustom passages, inlet location or branch starvation are uncertain. First planned CFD target.
Valves and quick disconnectsSupplier flow curves and valve positionPassage is custom, no adequate curve exists, or local cavitation/loading matters.
Heat exchangerSupplier hydraulic and thermal mapsDesigning its channels/distributor rather than selecting a commercial unit.
Cold plateOEM hydraulic and thermal mapsDesigning channels, conjugate heat transfer, chip hot spots or flow uniformity.
Air side and heat rejectionEnergy balance and fan/coil mapsRack recirculation, containment leakage or outdoor hot-air reingestion controls performance.

The likely high-loss candidates are narrow connectors, cold-plate channels, small hoses, throttled valves, dirty filters and exchanger passages. Their ranking requires actual curves and the solved critical circuit. A manifold may be thermally important because it distributes flow poorly even when its overall pressure drop is modest.

Component CFD will export Δp(flow, split, temperature) and thermal maps back into OpenModelica. Qualification checks include mass and energy balance, total-pressure loss, branch distribution, mesh/near-wall sensitivity and comparison with supplier or test data. Header and microchannel flow regimes must be assessed separately.

06 / Project development record

From a design basis to a tested system.

  1. Available

    Stage 01 · Public references and hydraulic design basis

    Conceptual architecture, flow/diameter comparison, provisional pressure budget, source-backed engineering plan and downloadable calculations. OpenModelica installation and a stock hydraulic software test are complete; the data-center network is not yet built.

  2. Next

    Stage 02 · Build the OpenModelica network

    Tagged P&ID and reviewable routing geometry; segmented supply/return headers, pump curves, ten rack resistances, valves and a pressure reference. First results: rack flows, pressures, critical circuit and pump operating point.

  3. Planned

    Stage 03 · System-level operating scenarios

    Compare load, ΔT, pipe size, uneven rack resistance, dirty filters, partial blockage and pump/standby response. Add thermal volumes, exchanger behavior and control/sensor dynamics after checking the steady hydraulic baseline.

  4. Planned

    Stage 04 · Distribution-manifold CFD

    FreeCAD geometry and OpenFOAM pressure/flow-distribution calculations on Intel. Compare inlet locations and branch geometry, then replace uncertain 1D losses with reviewed component maps.

  5. Planned

    Stage 05 · Coupled thermal and targeted CFD studies

    Resolve cold plates or air-side recirculation where needed; assess coolant temperature, residual air cooling and facility heat rejection. Propagate component changes into full-system pump and temperature predictions.

  6. Planned

    Stage 06 · Validation and design review

    Supplier curves and physical-loop measurements with instrument uncertainty, conservation checks and holdout cases. Water-hammer assessment requires suitable transient fidelity; a simple incompressible network does not establish peak surge pressure.
