Stage 06 / Leak detection, isolation and service recovery

Close both paths. Protect the remaining racks.

A supply valve alone does not contain an internal rack leak: the return header can feed it backward. Explore 21 saved OpenModelica pressure-and-flow solutions for an equivalent 1, 3 or 6 mm leak inside R05’s two isolation boundaries. This stage inherits the manually balanced network; it does not assume a PICV prevents reverse flow.

One valve can leave a live leak

For the assumed 3 mm opening, the unisolated leak is 7.14 L/min. Supply-only closure still loses 5.73 L/min through return backfeed. Return-only closure still loses 7.34 L/min from the supply.

Isolation changes the pump duty

Ideal two-sided closure stops sustained header feed. At unchanged speed, the healthy racks over-flow. A steady speed reset from 94.48% to 89.33% restores a healthy-rack minimum of 144.7 L/min, reducing estimated pump-bank input from 8.09 to 6.84 kW.

Scope: isothermal, quasi-static pressure/flow analysis with a maintained 300 kPa absolute reference pressure and assumed component maps. The source can supply unlimited makeup in this model; real coolant inventory depletion is not solved. R05 is unavailable after isolation: nine remaining 100 kW rack design duties, not an equal-capacity ten-rack energy saving. No physical leak, sensor, valve closure or pressure-surge validation.

Step by step: a protection concept to test

  1. Define the protected zone before choosing valves. Put an isolation boundary on both the supply and return serving the rack. Keep a drip tray and local leak sensing within that zone; retain accessible vent/drain points for controlled maintenance. A common header failure affects a larger zone. This study changes the R05 branch hydraulics without redesigning the Stage 02 CAD.
  2. Detect locally and supervise the signal. Tray/rope sensors detect escaped coolant; rack flow, supply/return imbalance, pressure and makeup trends can provide context. Their placement, coverage, fault detection and response time need selected hardware and tests. Local sensing can find a small leak that global flow-meter error masks.
  3. Coordinate rack power and coolant isolation. A leak alarm should invoke the reviewed electrical-and-cooling response for the affected equipment. A rack with its coolant cut off must not continue its assumed 100 kW load. Keep the unaffected racks supplied; a larger/common fault can require CDU or zone isolation. No thermal ride-through or electrical shutdown delay is modeled here.
  4. Close both boundaries and check the result. Supply-only closure leaves return backfeed; return-only closure leaves supply feed. A closed-position indication alone cannot establish a tight seal. The 1% residual-conductance fault demonstrates continued flow despite a nominal close command. Check valve feedback together with flow/leak status; escalation logic remains a design requirement.
  5. Reset pump duty for the surviving network. With both sides of R05 shut, the healthy racks rise to approximately 153.1–153.5 L/min at the commissioned speed. Reducing speed gives 144.7–145.1 L/min at 6.84 kW. This is a recalculated steady setpoint, not a simulated controller transition.
  6. Manage trapped liquid and qualify closure dynamics. Tight isolation stops sustained header feed, but stored coolant can still drain. Trapped sections also need a reviewed thermal-expansion/relief arrangement. Faster closure can reduce exposure while increasing pressure-surge risk. A compressible transient analysis and hardware test must establish pressure peaks and acceptable timing before deployment.

How much can escape before both valves seal?

The hole selector sets this screen’s equivalent opening. Qbound = CdA√[2(Pbound−Patm)/ρ], with Cd=0.62 and a 100%-speed shutoff pressure envelope of kPa absolute at the leak height. Hold that rate for detection + logic + slowest closure, then add a full-drain inventory ceiling. It assumes both valves actually seal and the source maintains pressure. It does not apply to a stuck/leaking valve’s continued feed; no finite containment volume solves an indefinitely supplied leak.

The inherited branch pipes hold approximately 5.97 L; equipment/manifold inventory is unknown. The default 10 L total drainable volume is an assumption, not a measured pan requirement. Concurrent closure is assumed; a sequential control scheme needs its actual total time. Surge can exceed this steady pressure envelope. Air ingress, drainage paths, spray capture, detection transport and real valve Kv-versus-time are not resolved.
