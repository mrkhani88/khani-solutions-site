Stage 05 / Manual balance versus pressure-independent valves

Hold the rack flow—until the available pressure runs out.

Compare manual valves with a generic pressure-independent control valve (PICV) in the same routed ten-rack network. Twenty-four native OpenModelica solutions cover six resistance scenarios and two pump policies. Both technologies start at 144.7 L/min per rack; manual valve settings remain fixed throughout.

Small changes: regulation can hold flow

With R05’s equipment resistance coefficient increased by 5% and commissioned pump speeds held, the manual case gives R05 L/min. The ideal PICV case keeps all racks at 144.700 L/min because every valve still has enough differential pressure.

Large changes: valves cannot create pressure

For R05 +20%, its fixed-speed PICV flow falls to L/min. Raising the pump to its 100% limit improves it to L/min, still below target. The flow limiter has opened fully; pump head is the remaining limit.

Model scope: ideal static regulation with a chosen 35 kPa minimum valve Δp and 5 kPa commissioning reserve. It is not a calibrated commercial valve. The manual reference needs 94.48% speed; the PICV reference needs 98.11%. This extra pressure costs power and must be included in the comparison.

Step by step: what changes and why

  1. Commission both systems at the same useful flow. Retain the Stage 04 manual coefficients and 94.48% speed. For PICVs, size pump head for the worst circuit plus 35 kPa valve operating differential and a 5 kPa reserve; this needs 98.11% speed. The estimated reference inputs are 8.190 kW manual versus 9.083 kW PICV. The difference includes the deliberately different valve head requirements, not an intrinsic efficiency ranking of all PICVs.
  2. Increase one rack’s equipment resistance. At +5%, the manual restriction does not readjust, so R05 loses flow and the other racks gain slightly. The ideal PICV opens farther, using available pressure reserve to keep its flow target. At +20%, R05 consumes more pressure than that reserve can supply; its PICV falls below the 35 kPa regulation threshold and flow drops.
  3. Load the common filter. Increase its reference pressure-loss coefficient from 20 to 25 or 40 kPa at nominal total flow. This is a quadratic loss map, so actual filter Δp also changes with solved flow. A moderate increase can be absorbed by PICV reserve. With a doubled coefficient, fixed-speed flows fall in both networks because every branch shares the extra common loss.
  4. Recalculate pump speed without changing manual presets. The manual policy seeks enough speed for the lowest-flow rack to reach target; other racks can then over-flow. The PICV policy seeks the worst path’s minimum valve pressure plus the 5 kPa reserve. These are recalculated steady setpoints, not simulated feedback controllers. Both are capped at 100% of the inherited assumed pump curve.
  5. Separate common loss from local imbalance. With only the filter doubled, the manual system recovers approximately 144.7 L/min at every rack at % speed and kW: every circuit received the same extra common resistance. The chosen PICVs require more minimum head and, at the 100% pump limit, still leave the lowest-flow rack at L/min. A suitable lower-Δp valve or a different pump duty could change that result.
  6. Check flow service before comparing watts. PICVs prevent excess flow while they regulate, but do not remove piping/filter loss or create head. This assumed manual system is lower-power at reference duty; the PICV system is more resistant to small branch changes. Large changes expose the selected pump/valve envelope. Increasing pump size, lowering verified valve minimum Δp or servicing the filter requires a separate design comparison.

With both severe disturbances together and the pump capped at 100%, the lowest rack receives L/min with manual valves versus L/min with the chosen PICV model. Neither serves every rack at target. This counterexample matters: a valve with a higher minimum pressure requirement can perform worse once the pump is head-limited.

The valve model and its limits

Pressure independence has an operating window

For positive valve differential pressure, the generic law is Q/Qtarget = min[1, √(Δp/35 kPa)]. Below 35 kPa, the valve is treated as fully open and flow falls. Above it, the ideal regulator holds its target. Every solved case is checked below the chosen 414 kPa upper-envelope guard.

The square-root fallback is an assumed approximation. Real droop, pressure-dependent accuracy, actuator dynamics, hysteresis, valve authority and sensor errors are not calibrated here.

Precision is numerical, not a hardware promise. The cited commercial data sheet specifies ±5% setpoint accuracy; at 144.7 L/min that is about ±7.2 L/min. The ideal plateau excludes that error, which can exceed the mild-change differences shown here. The scenarios are not statistical uncertainty bounds or product acceptance tests.
