Independent engineering case study

From public aircraft views to CFD.

Public-reference Blender geometry, two snappyHexMesh grids and steady OpenFOAM RANS expose pressure loading, curved airflow paths and preliminary component drag.

What I built and what this study answers

I built an editable exterior approximation of Archer Midnight from public photographs, multiple aircraft views, and published patent drawings. I then prepared a separate watertight surface for an initial, unpowered external-aerodynamics calculation. The aim was to examine pressure loading, airflow deflection, local reverse flow, and the relative drag contributions of the modeled components at one prescribed operating point.

This is an independent Khani Solutions portfolio demonstration associated with the methods used in my aerospace work. It is not a Wisk, Boeing, or Archer engineering deliverable, manufacturer CAD, a flight-test correlation, or a prediction of the production aircraft's performance. No employer geometry, battery details, internal aircraft data, or proprietary operating conditions were used. Archer Midnight and Wisk's aircraft are different designs.

The two calculations ended normally, but neither met every declared numerical acceptance target. The finer-grid force history still varied, and the drag coefficient differed between the two grids. Those limitations are part of the result: the video is a visualization of a preliminary solver field, while the analysis provides questions for the next model iteration.

Building the exterior from public references

Archer's published aircraft imagery supplied front, side, rear, flight and turntable views. Design patent USD1067164S1 supplied eight external shape views, while US11787551B1 provided context for boom-mounted electric engines and tilt architecture. Patent illustrations constrain visible arrangement and silhouette; they do not provide the complete, dimensioned aerodynamic surface or establish that every photograph depicts the same configuration.

The draft used 48 ft (14.6304 m) span and 33 ft (10.0584 m) length as nominal scale anchors. The cited FAA submission's full PDF returned an access error during reconstruction, so those figures were taken from its indexed excerpt and retained as a qualified input. An older Archer disclosure gave 47 ft span. I documented that discrepancy rather than treating both values as equivalent measurements of one configuration.

The fuselage was constructed as a cubic-Hermite loft through visually estimated longitudinal stations, with broader superellipse cabin sections, a raked forward profile, a flatter belly and a smooth aft taper. Glazing patches were projected onto the loft: a wraparound windshield, shaped pilot panes and passenger panes with rising aft edges. The landing gear used swept, rounded rectangular fairing lofts, wheel boots and a revised nose-gear shell. Leading- and trailing-edge shoulder fairings formed the estimated wing-to-cabin transition.

Three boom centerlines were placed on each side at approximately 1.78, 4.05 and 6.35 m from the center plane. The main-wing chord distribution was specified at seven spanwise stations. The wing section was a generic symmetric 13%-thickness profile; the V-tail used a generic 10%-thickness section. Actual aircraft camber, twist, incidence, airfoil coordinates, control settings, structural shape and flight trim were unavailable. These assumed sections are a major aerodynamic limitation, even when the exterior looks convincing.

The source includes a hover configuration and a forward-tilt cruise configuration. The CFD copy used cruise frame 120. The editable model is mesh-based exterior CAD, useful for studying visible shape and making controlled geometry edits; it is not a manufacturing-ready boundary representation, tolerance model, structural assembly or installation design.

Converting the visual CAD into a CFD surface

Intersecting visual components are unsuitable as an unexamined fluid boundary. I retained the fuselage, shoulder/root fairings, wings, V-tail, six booms, cruise-oriented forward nacelles and hubs, rear masts/hubs, and exposed landing gear. Propeller blades, decorative seams, window/door handles and separate glazing surface patches were removed. The underlying closed cabin surface remains; removing the glazing patches does not open the cabin.

A 25 mm voxel union with two smoothing passes produced one connected, closed exterior. Its triangulated export contained 516,004 triangles, zero non-manifold edges and a positive signed volume of 17.5358 m³. The cleaned surface extended approximately 10.505 m in X, 14.630 m across Y and 4.378 m in Z. Forward nacelles project ahead of the fuselage, so the full surface's X extent differs from the nominal fuselage length anchor.

The cleaning operation rounds small features and blunts thin trailing edges. Surface closure is a prerequisite for meshing, not evidence of geometric fidelity. Triangles were tagged as fuselage, wing, tail, booms, nacelles or gear using their nearest original component centroids. Labels near joined components are approximate; the maximum label-association distance was approximately 0.421 m. The force breakdown therefore has an additional component-boundary uncertainty.

Volume mesh generation and wall treatment

The external domain was X = −35 to 75 m, Y = 0 to 35 m and Z = −30 to 35 m. X points downstream, Y spans the aircraft, and Z is vertical. Only the Y ≥ 0 half was solved, with a symmetry boundary at Y = 0. The full aircraft in the video is the calculated half mirrored across that plane. This assumption excludes asymmetric inflow and asymmetric flow modes.

blockMesh created a 74 × 24 × 44 Cartesian background grid. snappyHexMesh then performed castellation, surface snapping and layer addition. Near-body and wake boxes received level-2 refinement. Distance refinement around the aircraft used level 4 within 0.18 m and level 3 within 0.65 m. Surface refinement was level 5 on most coarse-grid parts, with nacelles at level 6; the finer grid used level 6 on all named components. These are octree refinement levels, not statements that the mesh is uniformly resolved.

The snap controls included four patch-smoothing passes, tolerance 1.8, 50 solve iterations, eight relaxation iterations, 12 feature-snap iterations and implicit feature snapping. Five near-wall prism layers were requested, with expansion ratio 1.3, relative outer-layer thickness 0.4 and minimum relative thickness 0.08. Layer coverage and actual wall resolution must be assessed after meshing; requesting five layers does not guarantee five intact layers everywhere.

The larger out-of-range area on gear and booms is relevant when judging skin-friction and separation sensitivity. simpleFoam solves steady incompressible continuity and momentum with a modeled turbulent stress contribution. In compact notation, ∇·U = 0 and ∇·(U⊗U) = −∇(p/ρ) + ∇·[νeff(∇U + ∇Uᵀ)]. The k–ω SST model closes the RANS system through turbulence transport equations for turbulent kinetic energy k and specific dissipation rate ω. Its standard implementation and coefficients were retained; no transition model, LES or resolved rotor dynamics was added.

The inlet velocity was fixed. The outlet used an inletOutlet velocity condition to accommodate local reversal and fixed zero gauge pressure. The outer farfield used freestreamVelocity and freestreamPressure. The symmetry plane used symmetryPlane conditions; the aircraft used noSlip velocity and zeroGradient pressure. Inlet k and ω were fixed, with inletOutlet conditions at outlet/farfield. Wall conditions used kqRWallFunction, omegaWallFunction and nutkWallFunction.

OpenFOAM's incompressible pressure field has units of m²/s²: it is kinematic pressure, not pressure directly in pascals. Dimensional pressure differences are obtained by multiplying by density. The turbulence initialization used k = 1.5(U∞I)² and ω = √k/(Cμ1/4 L), with Cμ = 0.09. Density was supplied explicitly as rhoInf = 1.225 for the dimensional force integration.

The moment reference was (−1.5, 0, 2.3) m, an arbitrary study point rather than the aircraft center of gravity. Drag points along +X, lift along +Z and pitch about Y. Half-domain dimensional forces were doubled for the full-airframe estimates; coefficients used the half reference area consistently. These choices do not provide a pitching-moment or trim assessment about a known aircraft CG.

Discretization, linear solvers and convergence targets

The time-derivative scheme was steadyState. Gradients used Gauss linear, with a cellLimited Gauss linear 1 velocity gradient. Momentum convection used bounded Gauss linearUpwindV grad(U); k and ω convection used bounded Gauss upwind. Laplacians and normal gradients used limited 0.5 non-orthogonal correction; interpolation was linear. This combination favors a robust screening calculation but does not remove mesh or turbulence-model uncertainty.

Pressure used GAMG with a GaussSeidel smoother, absolute linear tolerance 10⁻⁸ and relative tolerance 0.01. Velocity, k and ω used smoothSolver with symGaussSeidel smoothing, absolute tolerance 10⁻⁸ and relative tolerance 0.05. SIMPLE used one non-orthogonal corrector, with the consistent option disabled. potentialFoam initialization used six non-orthogonal correctors. The parallel decomposition method was scotch with 12 subdomains.

Initial under-relaxation was p = 0.3, U = 0.65 and k/ω = 0.6. After finer-grid iteration 510, these were reduced to p = 0.15, U = 0.25 and k/ω = 0.4, and the run was extended to iteration 1400. The coarse run ended at iteration 1000. The stored solver “time” is a SIMPLE iteration index; deltaT = 1 in the control dictionary does not make each iteration one physical second.

The declared post-run acceptance targets were final initial equation residuals ≤ 10⁻⁴ for p and ≤ 10⁻⁵ for the other equations; a last-100-iteration Cd peak-to-peak range ≤ 1% of its mean; a Cl range ≤ 0.002; and relative net boundary volume flux below 10⁻⁴. Linear-solver tolerances measure convergence of an individual matrix solution, while these residual/force checks assess the nonlinear iteration. They are different tests.

What the colors and moving arrows mean

The body colors show local pressure coefficient, Cp. Cp = (p − p∞)/(½ρU∞²). With this kinematic-pressure field and zero outlet gauge reference, Cp = pkinematic/(½U∞²). Red is toward the higher-pressure end of the displayed scale; blue is toward the lower-pressure end. The range is fixed at −1.5 to +1.0. Values outside it saturate: the actual final snapshot spans approximately −4.00 to +1.09 across the tagged aircraft surfaces. The palette's pale midpoint should not be read as exactly Cp = 0; use the numeric legend.

High pressure near a forward-facing region is consistent with flow deceleration and stagnation. Low-pressure regions can accompany local acceleration around curved surfaces and junctions. Pressure forces depend on both pressure and surface orientation. A red patch is not automatically the largest drag source, and a blue patch is not proof of useful lift. Integrating pressure and viscous traction over the surface is required to obtain the total load.

Cp and Cd are different quantities. Cp is a local surface-pressure measure. Cd = D/(q∞S) is the dimensionless integrated drag coefficient for a specified reference area. Cd is not the colored variable in this film. Skin-friction drag is also included in the force calculation, although it is not represented by the Cp color map.

The cyan lines show selected native streamlines around the fuselage and over the wings. Curvature indicates how the modeled geometry deflects the computed velocity field. Arrowheads point along interpolated U. Their display motion is slowed and synchronized for readability; arrow count, apparent spacing and line thickness do not measure mass flow, velocity magnitude or boundary-layer thickness. Straight bypass paths were deliberately excluded to expose the near-aircraft behavior.

The selection started with 409 seeds placed upstream of the cabin, near upper/lower wing leading edges and in spatially separated reverse-flow cells. Bidirectional adaptive RK45 integration used the reconstructed finer-grid native U field: initial step 0.012 m, minimum 0.001 m, maximum 0.035 m, 5,000 maximum steps, maximum integration length 14 m, terminal speed 0.1 m/s and error tolerance 10⁻⁶. Cell velocity was interpolated to points for tracing.

Candidate body paths required at least 0.20 m bending and 18° accumulated turning; wing paths required 0.045 m and 12°. Local wake candidates required at least 160° turning and 12% reverse-flow samples. Cropping and spacing reduced 766 candidate segments to nine half-aircraft paths—three body, three wing and three local-wake paths—then mirrored them to 18 displayed paths. No extra curvature or synthetic vortex geometry was inserted.

The purple paths are local circulation candidates. They show substantial turning and reverse flow around modeled gear/junction regions in this solver iterate. A derivative-based Q = ½(||Ω||² − ||S||²) check was examined as supporting evidence, but no Q isosurface is displayed. Positive Q alone, boundary-sensitive derivatives and a curling streamline in an unconverged steady field do not independently establish a coherent vortex, its strength, shedding frequency or stability.

The ten-second film is not ten seconds of aircraft flight or transient CFD. The pressure and velocity fields stay fixed while the camera moves and markers traverse the calculated paths. It is encoded at 1920 × 1080 and 24 fps. The first 180° camera sweep reveals the opposite side; the camera then continues another 180° back to the opening view. Camera position, target, zoom and orientation close over the period, and arrows complete integer traversals so their phases also repeat. Added periodic airflow sound is presentation design, not a simulated acoustic result.

Why the study matters and what to test next

An exterior reconstruction becomes useful when it connects a visible feature to a testable aerodynamic question. The pressure map locates regions worth examining; bent streamlines expose interference between nearby surfaces; integrated component forces help organize the next controlled comparison. This is more informative than judging shape from a shaded CAD render alone.

  1. Gear and fairings: compare the same operating point with improved fairing transitions or a representative cruise gear configuration. The modeled gear is the largest drag group and includes local reversed-flow behavior; a fairing benefit still requires a new, converged comparison.
  2. Cabin and shoulder shaping: examine the forward cabin cross-section and wing/cabin transitions. At the final snapshot, an X = −3 to −1 m fuselage band contributed about 306 N of pressure drag, while the aft X ≥ 3 m band contributed about 3 N. These bands are diagnostics of this draft rather than isolated design sensitivities.
  3. Nacelle/mast/hub transitions: test the blunt interfaces and junctions before assuming a boom or airfoil change is the dominant improvement. The same snapshot placed about 235 N of nacelle-group pressure drag in the X = −1 to +1 m region.
  4. Establish lifting geometry: replace generic sections with a justified airfoil/incidence/twist model and solve a meaningful lift/trim condition before comparing L/D, range, power or aircraft efficiency.

No modified design was solved in this study, so no drag reduction, range gain or optimization success is claimed. A sound next stage would repair flagged mesh regions, improve layer coverage, resolve the residual/force behavior, separate geometry and grid changes, add further systematically refined grids, and compare against appropriate measurements. Powered operation would require a justified propulsor representation; time-resolved wake questions would require a suitable transient method and its own temporal-resolution study.

The useful outcome here is a repeatable public-data workflow and a transparent preliminary flow field. Its engineering value is in identifying what to inspect and what evidence to collect next, while keeping the attractive animation separate from the level of numerical and geometric confidence.
