01 / Define the system

One repeatable cooling module. Two separate liquid circuits.

The reference design transfers 1 MW into liquid across ten parallel racks. Each rack has an assumed 100 kW liquid load; residual air-cooled heat is a separate requirement. These are independent study assumptions, not AWS or Google operating specifications.

Facility-water system · FWS

Heat-rejection plant → CDU heat exchanger → heat-rejection plant. Climate, water availability, heat reuse and approach temperatures determine the dry-cooler, economizer or chiller configuration.

Technology-cooling system · TCS

CDU exchanger → pump bank → supply header → rack manifolds and parallel server paths → return header → exchanger. The exchanger transfers heat while the two coolant streams remain separate.

A liquid-to-air sidecar is a future topology comparison. It transfers heat to the room air system; it does not eliminate the building's heat-rejection duty. “Modular” here means a repeatable cooling block, rather than a claim about a particular containerized facility.

Equations, assumptions and what the calculator omits

Flow: ṁ = Q̇/(cp ΔT); q = ṁ/ρ; v = 4q/(πD²).

Loss: Δp = [fL/D + ΣK] ρv²/2. The calculation uses the Darcy friction factor: 64/Re in laminar flow and the Haaland approximation in turbulent flow. Transitional flow requires further treatment.

Pump: H = Δp/(ρg); Pelectric = qΔp/η. Parallel pumps share flow, not head. The critical path includes common piping and one rack's circulation path; parallel-rack losses are not added in series.

Missing at this stage: segmented header flow balance, actual pump curves, flow-dependent component maps, temperature-dependent fluid properties, unequal rack resistance, control dynamics, pump failure, thermal inventory, water hammer and physical calibration. High velocities are flagged as a screening concern, not as a universal code limit.

The dynamic pressure estimate does not establish a component pressure rating. A rating must account for fill pressure, pump shutoff, elevation, thermal expansion and transients.

Step by step: how the new operating point is calculated

  1. Keep the accepted baseline. The Stage 03 network is reproduced before changing anything: 152.799–154.996 L/min per rack and 9.714 kW for the duty-pump bank. The native baseline agrees with the previously saved result.
  2. Reduce speed to match total flow. At 94.31% speed, total flow reaches approximately 24.117 L/s. Individual racks still receive 144.089–146.168 L/min because direct-return header paths have different losses. Matching the sum does not independently set ten branch flows.
  3. Calculate balancing resistance at equal flow. Set each branch to 144.7 L/min in the independent sizing calculation. Solve every header segment at its actual remaining flow. R-10 has the highest unvalved circuit loss. Keep an assumed 15 kPa actual drop at its balancing valve; add the difference in path loss to the other rack valves. The reduced-speed valve drops are 15.00–18.87 kPa.
  4. Solve speed and valves together. Match the pump curve to the critical-circuit requirement, then rerun the native network with those speed and valve settings. Both duty pumps run at 94.48%; all ten racks meet the target. This is the minimum speed only within the fixed topology and declared 15 kPa critical-valve floor. It is not a global pump or valve optimization.
  5. Compare at the same delivered flow. Full-speed balancing also achieves the rack targets, but consumes 9.560 kW. Reducing speed with balancing consumes 8.190 kW: about 1.371 kW less at equal flow. Speed-only uses about 41 W less than the combined case, but leaves the critical rack below target. Calculate power from Δp × Q / (0.75 × 0.90); the cube law is not used as the result.
  6. Check what happens when the system changes. With speed and valve settings held fixed, increasing only R05’s equipment resistance coefficient by 20% reduces its flow to about 135.53 L/min; other racks rise to about 145.25 L/min. This is a separate steady sensitivity case, not a blockage transient. It shows why a fixed manual balance needs review when circuit resistance changes.
Equations, acceptance checks and engineering limits

For equal target flows, define Lᵢ as common + supply-header + return-header + rack losses excluding its balancing valve. Choose Δppump = max(Lᵢ) + 15 kPa, then Δpvalve,i = Δppump − Lᵢ. Convert the actual drop to each quadratic valve’s nominal-flow coefficient. With r = Q / Qnom and speed fraction s, solve the inherited assumed pump curve H = 37s² − 0.5sr − 9r² [m]. Native Modelica.Fluid pipes and fittings then solve the flows without imposed branch-flow sources.

All five native cases reached their final export time with finite results. Accepted checks include mass continuity, loop-pressure closure, pump-work numerical energy closure, reproduction of Stage 03, balanced-rack error <0.001 L/min and independent exact-Colebrook flow agreement <0.001%. The reduced-speed cases use direct nonlinear initialization after the default homotopy attempt failed. Accepted runtime logs and prior diagnostic attempts are downloadable; the compiler’s redundant-start/nominal alias warning is retained. The 0–1 s export repeats a steady solution; it is not a physical one-second operating test.

These are numerical checks of the assumed network, not experimental validation. Pipe IDs and routing are inherited; device curves, fitting K values and efficiencies remain provisional. No valve actuator dynamics, pressure-controller stability, physical heat-exchanger performance, water hammer, NPSH qualification or standby transition has been solved. The 15 kPa static-valve floor must not be reused as a pressure-independent valve’s required operating differential.

Flow-balancing approaches / Literature → model evidence → next decision

Balance the branches. Control the pressure they actually need.

The references below explain established hydronic methods. Our numerical cases test this particular independent network; they do not establish AWS/Google practice or qualify a commercial device. Manual balancing is the first implementable model stage. Dynamic alternatives need device operating ranges and control models before a fair energy comparison.

Why start with speed plus static balancing?

Our baseline is already fairly uniform: only 1.43% rack spread. Most of this stage’s power reduction comes from removing overall over-flow, while balancing makes each rack meet its target. The combined case uses slightly more power than speed-only because it serves the critical rack rather than just the average.

Where the next analysis should go

Use measured or supplier rack/valve curves, then compare manual settings against a real PICV or rack-flow controller under unequal loads, filter loading and rack isolation. Add critical-branch pressure sensing to reset pump speed. CFD is most useful for uncertain tee/manifold losses; it is not needed to calculate a known valve’s Kv.

Engineering method / Turn estimates into a design

Choose the route and components together.

Pipe size, length and bends

Compare 125/40 mm header/branch clear IDs against 150/50 mm. Actual DN/NPS size depends on wall thickness, ports and fittings. The Stage 01 calculator retains its original 40/20 m placeholders; Stages 03–04 use the measured Stage 02 CAD routes.

Use accessible service zones, compatible materials, standard long-radius elbows where practical, pipe supports, drains, high-point vents and hose bend-radius control. Compare direct return and reverse return; neither layout removes the need to balance rack flows.

Elevation and pump suction

In a filled closed loop, upward and downward hydrostatic heads cancel around the circuit for equal density. Five metres of elevation does not automatically add five metres of circulating pump head.

It still changes local pressure by approximately 49 kPa. Check highest-point pressure, lowest-point rating, fill/expansion pressure, air removal, and available NPSH using absolute suction pressure and coolant vapor pressure. Open-reservoir transfer adds net static head.

Pressure loss alone cannot select the exchanger. For an illustrative 25/35 °C facility stream and 35/45 °C technology stream, a 1 MW counterflow duty implies approximately 100 kW/K UA before fouling and pump heat. A dry cooler cannot deliver 25 °C water in 35 °C outdoor air; site climate may require warmer coolant or mechanical cooling.

Coolant chemistry, wetted metals, seals and water-quality limits must follow the selected equipment. The water-property assumption is not a chemistry specification. Check the outdoor facility loop separately for freeze protection.

Modeling fidelity / Spend CFD effort where it changes a decision

Solve the network in 1D. Resolve uncertain geometry in 3D.

Initial modeling method and escalation criteria
ComponentInitial methodCFD becomes useful when…
Straight pipe / standard elbowDarcy–Weisbach and tested fitting KCross-section is unusual or closely spaced bends create interacting separation.
Tees and distribution manifoldRun/branch correlations dependent on flow splitCustom passages, inlet location or branch starvation are uncertain. First planned CFD target.
Valves and quick disconnectsSupplier flow curves and valve positionPassage is custom, no adequate curve exists, or local cavitation/loading matters.
Heat exchangerSupplier hydraulic and thermal mapsDesigning its channels/distributor rather than selecting a commercial unit.
Cold plateOEM hydraulic and thermal mapsDesigning channels, conjugate heat transfer, chip hot spots or flow uniformity.
Air side and heat rejectionEnergy balance and fan/coil mapsRack recirculation, containment leakage or outdoor hot-air reingestion controls performance.

The likely high-loss candidates are narrow connectors, cold-plate channels, small hoses, throttled valves, dirty filters and exchanger passages. Their ranking requires actual curves and the solved critical circuit. A manifold may be thermally important because it distributes flow poorly even when its overall pressure drop is modest.

Component CFD will export Δp(flow, split, temperature) and thermal maps back into OpenModelica. Qualification checks include mass and energy balance, total-pressure loss, branch distribution, mesh/near-wall sensitivity and comparison with supplier or test data. Header and microchannel flow regimes must be assessed separately.
