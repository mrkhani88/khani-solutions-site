Thermal / External exhaust

Thermal-runaway exhaust plume around a grounded aircraft

A reconstructed aircraft center section and a prescribed hot-gas pulse show how exhaust interacts with the fuselage and ground, spreads, and disperses after venting stops.

FreeCADGmshOpenFOAM2D buoyant flow

Independent demonstration · Assumed 600°C, 40 m/s slot release for 10 s, followed by 5 s dispersion. Approximate Midnight-inspired center section; no manufacturer battery or vent data.

More details — full technical case studyFewer details — close technical case study

The engineering challenge

Routing battery vent gas outside an aircraft is only part of the problem. Once a hot jet leaves the outlet, its momentum, buoyancy, the fuselage shape, and the nearby ground determine where it travels. A downward-facing vent can move the initial release away from the cabin yet create a hot region below the aircraft. The plume can spread along the ground, entrain surrounding air, and turn upward as it loses momentum.

I built this external-flow study to make those interactions visible for a stationary aircraft on the ground. The purpose is to establish a repeatable geometry-to-results workflow for examining a hypothetical thermal-runaway exhaust release. The model helps frame questions about outlet placement, clearance, local recirculation, and the persistence of hot gas after venting stops.

This is an independent Khani Solutions portfolio demonstration using an approximate Archer Midnight-inspired body reconstructed from public images. The outlet and release conditions are my assumptions. It is not an Archer or Wisk design analysis, employer project, certification result, or assessment of either company's battery safety.

What was modeled

The calculation resolves only the air outside a vertical, longitudinal aircraft center section. A prescribed hot-gas boundary represents exhaust that has already left the battery system. Cell failure, pack pressure, gas generation, propagation, and the internal vent duct are not solved. Consequently, the simulation predicts external flow and gas temperature for the specified release; it does not predict whether thermal runaway occurs or what a real pack would discharge.

Thermal-runaway exhaust can involve composition changes, combustible gases and particles. Here it is represented as a single nonreacting, air-like gas. The colored field is gas temperature, rather than a species concentration, flame, smoke-density field, or toxic-exposure measure.

Step 1 — Reconstruct and review the center section

I compared public side, front three-quarter, rear three-quarter, and elevated views of the black Midnight aircraft. An early side outline included features that lie outside the center plane. I replaced it with an estimated three-dimensional fuselage envelope built from 17 cross-sectional stations in FreeCAD, then intersected that envelope at the lateral coordinate y = 0. This produces a nose-to-tail center cut rather than using the entire projected side silhouette as a solid CFD obstruction.

The estimated fuselage is 7.2 m long. Image scaling uses an assumed 8.2 m projected whole-aircraft nose-to-tail-tip extent. Those dimensions, hidden contours, cross-sectional widths, and an assumed centered nose fairing are reconstruction inputs, not manufacturer measurements. The wing attachment is blended into the roof estimate. Off-center main landing-gear fairings, wing and rotor booms, and V-tail arms are excluded from the solved plane.

I overlaid the center cut on the reference image and reviewed the estimated envelope from multiple views before meshing. The approved 25 mm clearance beneath the assumed nose fairing was retained; the model does not establish actual tire contact. CAD validity, section planarity, and the exposed 100 mm vent edge were checked. The native FreeCAD and STEP geometry uses millimetres; the CFD geometry uses metres.

Step 3 — Generate and check the external-fluid mesh

Gmsh 4.15.2 generated the external-air mesh around the approved section. The wall was simplified to remove very short construction segments while preserving the vent endpoints. Maximum boundary deviation from the CAD cut was approximately 0.952 mm. The mesh contains 13,472 finite-volume cells and eight faces across the outlet, with a single cell through the span.

Target sizes were 12.5 mm near the vent, 6 mm around the nose-fairing gap, approximately 80 mm near the body, 100 mm in the near-ground release zone, 230 mm in the wider plume region, and up to 1.5 m in the far field. The local targets focus resolution on the source, clearances, and initial ground interaction. No dedicated wall-normal boundary-layer stack was established; wall functions were used, and wall-resolution independence was not demonstrated.

OpenFOAM's checkMesh -allTopology -allGeometry returned Mesh OK. Maximum non-orthogonality was 44.54° and maximum skewness was 1.57. These checks support a usable numerical mesh; they do not establish grid-independent plume temperatures.

Step 4 — Set up the flow and thermal solver

I used OpenCFD OpenFOAM v2506 buoyantPimpleFoam for transient compressible, buoyant, nonreacting flow with sensible-enthalpy transport. Density follows the perfect-gas equation of state, allowing the hot exhaust to be less dense than ambient air. The gas molecular weight is 28.965 kg/kmol, with constant specific heat Cp = 1,005 J/(kg·K). Sutherland viscosity uses As = 1.458 × 10⁻⁶ and Ts = 110.4 K. This is an air-property approximation across the imposed temperature range.

Turbulence is modeled using RANS k–omega SST. At the release boundary, k = 6 m²/s² and omega = 638.9 s⁻¹; initial ambient values are k = 10⁻⁶ m²/s² and omega = 1 s⁻¹. Aircraft and ground use kqRWallFunction, omegaWallFunction, nutkWallFunction, and the compressible thermal wall function. Turbulence closure and the 2D assumption influence entrainment and vortex behavior.

The aircraft and ground impose zero velocity and zero temperature gradient. The outer boundary uses pressureInletOutletVelocity, inletOutlet temperature with 25°C on inflow, and prghTotalPressure with a hydrostatically initialized pressure profile. Fixed-flux pressure conditions are used on walls and the mouth. The initial field is hydrostatic, rather than uniform pressure under gravity.

Step 5 — Run startup, release, and dispersion

Before injecting hot gas, I ran an independent 0.25 s quiet-atmosphere check. Its maximum speed was 0.000221 m/s, indicating that hydrostatic initialization did not create a substantial artificial background flow. The release calculation then ran serially to 0.25 s, followed by a six-rank pilot to 0.5 s and continuation to 10 s. At that point the mouth was converted to a closed wall, and the calculation continued to 15 s.

Both the release and dispersion phases ended normally. Every saved temperature, velocity, pressure, and density field was checked for finite values, with positive pressure and density and accepted temperature bounds. Inlet mass flux, outer-boundary mass flux, and total fluid mass were monitored every solver timestep, producing 124,652 samples. The largest monitored step was approximately 0.000963 s.

Step 6 — Audit mass balance and interpret the results

The imposed jet initially carries hot gas aft and toward the ground. The evolving velocity field shows ground interaction, spreading and recirculation, while buoyancy and entrainment alter the plume as it slows. After source closure, the nearby temperature field decreases as hot gas mixes and moves away. The video shows these calculated processes for this particular pulse, geometry, and still-air scenario.

Step 7 — Create a readable scientific video

I rendered the native temperature and velocity results into a 10-second, 1600 × 900 H.264 video at 30 fps. The 15 physical seconds play at 1.5× speed. A white background, Turbo scale from 30–600°C, and dark instantaneous streamlines keep the flow visible. Gas below 30°C is white, and 30–40°C blends into white. Spatial and temporal interpolation smooth the display; reported numerical metrics use native fields.

The fixed view covers longitudinal x = −3 to 14 m and height −0.1 to 8 m. Plume outside that window is cropped from the video but remains part of the CFD domain. The nose points right for agreement with the reference. Streamlines describe the instantaneous velocity field; they are not trajectories of tracked gas particles.

The pencil aircraft overlay is an AI-assisted visual derived from the public side reference and aligned using a uniform scale and landmarks. It supplies recognizable aircraft context. Off-center gear, booms, and tail features visible in the sketch are not CFD obstacles. The approved center-cut boundary remains the solved wall. Image generation supplied the presentation overlay only; temperature and velocity come from OpenFOAM. Plain-section and photo-based variants were also made, and all three videos were fully decoded and checked for their 300 frames and 10-second duration.

How this helps engineering decisions

A workflow like this can support early comparisons of vent location, direction, release history, aircraft clearance, ambient wind, and surrounding obstacles. It can help choose where to place thermocouples and flow diagnostics in a later physical test, or identify regions needing a coupled solid-heating model. Those comparisons have not been run here; this study establishes one screening case and its audit trail.

For design use, the next steps are measured time-dependent mass flow, temperature, composition and pressure; verified aircraft and vent geometry; three-dimensional flow; mesh, time-step and domain studies; turbulence and wind sensitivity; and comparison with a representative release experiment. Chemistry, radiation, particles, species transport, rotor flow, and solid conduction should be added where the engineering question requires them. Exposure limits, thermal-damage limits and evacuation distances require additional evidence.

The battery cooling study elsewhere in this portfolio addresses a different question: how generated heat moves through a module and its coolant circuit during an imposed operating load. The plume study starts at an assumed external vent source. Neither demonstration predicts cell failure or thermal-runaway propagation, and they are not coupled simulations.

Thermal / Battery cooling

Battery cooling with two heat exchangers in series

A 30-cell equivalent module, titanium casing, and two liquid cold plates in series show how internal thermal resistance and coolant warming affect transient cooling.

FreeCADElmerConjugate heat transfer

Independent demonstration · 300 W load, water at 1 L/min and 25°C inlet. Assumed cell properties and laminar screening model.

More details — full technical case studyFewer details — close technical case study

The challenge and why simulate it

Battery cooling must move heat from the module interior through interfaces and into the coolant. Low through-plane conductivity, local heat generation, cold-plate coverage and coolant warming can produce temperature differences that a single bulk-temperature estimate misses.

I built this independent thermal screening example to examine a series cooling architecture: two cold plates cool opposite faces, while the second plate receives water already warmed by the first. It illustrates why coolant temperature alone does not establish the hottest module temperature, and why transient heat storage must be included when interpreting cooling performance.

This is a Khani Solutions portfolio demonstration using assumed materials and operating conditions. It is not proprietary Wisk or Boeing hardware, an employer analysis, or a validated aircraft battery design. It is an operating-load cooling model, not a thermal-runaway initiation, propagation or exhaust calculation.

Workflow, model and results

I built this conjugate heat transfer study using FreeCAD for the geometry and Elmer for the flow and thermal calculations.

The model represents 30 pouch cells as a single anisotropic thermal block, measuring 352 × 188 × 169 mm, inside a 2 mm titanium casing. Two aluminum cold plates sit on opposite faces, separated from the casing by 0.5 mm thermal interface layers. Each plate contains a five-pass serpentine channel. Coolant passes through the first plate, crosses to the second, and then exits.

The battery conductivity was assumed to be 20 W/m·K in-plane and 1 W/m·K through-plane. That difference matters: a cold plate can remain relatively cool while the module interior heats up because heat cannot reach the cooled faces quickly enough.

The simulation used:

  • 300 W of uniform module heat generation.
  • 1 L/min water at 25°C, with all regions initially at 25°C.
  • Approximately 2.08 million tetrahedral elements.
  • A steady incompressible flow calculation followed by transient conduction and coolant advection, using 30-second steps over one hour.

At one hour, the calculated module peak reached 63.5°C, while the coolant reached 26.4°C after the first plate and 27.8°C at the outlet. The coolant was removing approximately 195 W at that instant; the remaining heat was still accumulating in the assembly. This was a transient result, not thermal equilibrium.

Practical design concerns include warmer coolant entering the second plate, pressure losses through both serpentine circuits, insufficient pump flow, channel blockage, and thermal interface gaps or uneven compression. The interfaces here were perfectly bonded; those defects were not simulated.

This is an illustrative screening study. The flow regime is transitional, although modeled as laminar. A mass-conserving flux reconstruction changed the calculated velocity field by about 22%, so flow refinement, turbulence sensitivity, mesh/time-step studies, and experimental comparison are needed before using these temperatures for design decisions.

The video compresses one simulated hour into 10 seconds.

What to do with these results

Use this example to frame architecture and test questions: whether a parallel coolant arrangement changes temperature uniformity, how flow rate affects heat removal and pumping demand, and how interface resistance or cell-to-cell heat variation affects the hotspot. Those variations and defects were not calculated in this run. The imposed 300 W load remains an input, rather than a prediction of electrochemical heat generation.

Design qualification would require representative material and interface measurements, adequate flow resolution, turbulence sensitivity where appropriate, mesh and time-step convergence, and comparison with module and circuit test data. A relatively cool outlet is not proof of a cool module core. A one-hour transient result is not a demonstrated steady operating limit.

Connection to the external plume study

Cooling and venting belong to different parts of battery thermal engineering. This model follows heat conduction and coolant transport in a module assembly. The aircraft plume study follows a hypothetical external hot-gas source after discharge. The two demonstrations share a focus on thermal-fluid behavior but are not coupled and do not establish thermal-runaway prevention.
