Cold start, realistic response models and a reserved heater margin.

02 / Follow the energy and feedback step by step

Why a cold start asks for more power.

Housing temperature→Sensor lag→PI + command ceiling→Command dead time→Delivered-power response→Housing heat + DC-bus demand
  1. Warm the thermal mass. The four motor-assembly capacities total 59 kJ/K. Raising all four from −40 to −5°C would store about 2.065 MJ (0.574 kWh), before accounting for concurrent motor losses, external losses and heat exchanged with the surrounding vehicle. The heater acts on the housing, so the four parts do not warm uniformly.
  2. Test the existing controller honestly. At startup the 35 K error requests 10.5 kW through its proportional term. The installed heater can deliver only 300 W. Anti-windup manages the integrator; it cannot supply unavailable heat. The ideal cold case and delayed cold case both spend about 2.6 h at the hardware limit.
  3. Add the feedback and output dynamics. Sensor temperature follows the housing gradually; the command arrives late and delivered power ramps. Their nominal few-second effect is small compared with an hours-long warm-up, but long sensor/output lags reduce damping around the target.
  4. Trade warm-up time for reserve. The 270 W ceiling keeps actual power 30 W below installed capacity and increases acquisition time by 26.7 min. Its slightly lower first-day heat use reflects spending longer cold, with smaller heat loss; it is not evidence of a more efficient heater.
  5. Retune the combined slow-response case. With the original gains, day 08 reaches 299.8 W and the maximum heater-on true-temperature error is 0.528 K, outside the chosen band. Reducing gains damps the night oscillation; Ki=0.5 leaves a larger warm-transition error. The Kp=200 / Ki=0.75 candidate brings the day 08 peak to 240.5 W and heater-on maximum error to 0.428 K. Inspect both the startup and settled windows before selecting a controller.
  6. Account for the whole system. Delivered heater power enters the motor housing and the common DC demand. Changed converter/battery losses feed back into the integrated 24-node thermal network. Cold battery charging permission remains active in the entire-network sensitivity, but prescribed hardware loads are not proven operable at −40°C.

02 / Explain the control mechanism

Remove the offset without increasing heater capacity.

  1. Recognize proportional droop. Stage 03 used P=30·(target−temperature). Supplying about 169 W therefore requires about 5.6 K of cold error. Spare heater capacity alone cannot remove that error.
  2. Increase Kp as a controlled comparison. Raising gain to 300 W/K reduces the minimum-temperature error to 0.67 K, but it still needs a nonzero error to sustain heat.
  3. Add an integral correction. Repeated cold error builds a holding-power term. Near the target, that term supplies the heat that offsets environmental losses while the proportional error becomes small.
  4. Limit delivered power and prevent windup. Clamp the heater to 0–300 W. Feed the difference between limited output and raw command back into the integrator, so it accounts for saturation and recovers rather than accumulating an unavailable command.
  5. Choose from the tested gains. With Kp fixed at 300 W/K, the selected Ki=2 keeps all nominal heater-on records within the chosen ±0.5 K band. Slower settings have larger warm-transition tracking errors; compare them instead of assuming any integral term fixes the whole cycle.
  6. Keep the energy consequence visible. Holding the motor warmer takes additional heat. Motor heating rises from 1.595 to 1.955 kWh/day. All-heater energy rises from 2.420 to 2.761 kWh/day: +0.340 kWh/day, or 14.1%. The original lower consumption accompanied a cold target shortfall.
e = Ttarget − Tsensor
u = Kp·e + I
Pheater = clip(u, 0, Pmax)
dI/dt = Ki·e + (Pheater − u) / 60 s

Positive e requests heat. I is a controller correction in watts, not thermal energy. P and I terms may individually exceed the cap and cancel; only Pheater enters the housing energy balance. The Modelica limited-PI documentation describes the general use of output limits and anti-windup; this downloadable model exposes its own equations explicitly.

Colder air and sensor lag retain heater headroom.

With air 10 K colder, the selected PI case reaches -5.14°C minimum and 240.5 W peak demand, leaving 59.5 W below the same 300 W cap. Earth/sky IR and wind/load forcing stay fixed, so this is an air-temperature sensitivity rather than a complete cold environmental bound.

01 / Define the problem before optimizing

What did we assume?

Sceye publicly describes onboard computers, sensors, solar/battery power and under-platform payloads. Its design partner describes propulsion and embedded-system involvement. A telecom antenna is also public. The cited sources do not disclose an avionics board BOM, RF heat map, motor loss map or insulation stack, so those numerical details stay explicit assumptions.

Insulation is a heat path, not a color.

Stage02 used an effective battery case-to-jacket conductance of2W/K, without selecting an insulation product or thickness. For scale, k=0.020 W/mK over 5 m² needs 50 mm to give2W/K even before seam and cable leaks. An 80 mm layer would give 1.25W/K; adding an assumed 0.75W/K bypass returns 2 W/K. Neither example closes insulation mass or proves installation feasibility.

A reflective outer layer, foam/aerogel blanket and low-conductivity mounts are candidates. Their installed conductivity must be measured versus temperature, pressure, compression and moisture.20km air at about5.5kPa is not vacuum; vacuum-MLI properties cannot replace gas conduction and thin-air convection.

Targets, limits and margins are different.

Heater control targets are3°C avionics tray,8°C battery case,0°C payload plate and−5°C motor housing. They are chosen control settings, not component survival or operating limits. The processor, RF source and motor winding can differ substantially from their heater sensors.

The motor housing is a bearing-temperature proxy only. Battery charging logic is illustrative and is not a qualified lithium-sulfur or lithium-ion charging limit. Real selections require supplier data and altitude/temperature testing.

02 / Controlled design changes

Why each change affects temperature and energy

  1. Reproduce the reference. Same24 thermal masses, surfaces, electrical loads, environment, heater gains and capacities. With switch control disabled, the new source reproduces Stage02 within0.001K on the common final-day grid.
  2. Increase heater capacity alone. Raise avionics/payload/motor caps from 100/120/100W to300/360/300W. More electrical heat raises cold temperatures, but the continuously connected radiator still loses heat.
  3. Reduce cold radiator coupling. Replace each fixed strap by an ideal temperature-responsive thermal switch. At low base temperature it conducts10% of its open value; coupling rises smoothly to 100% over15K. Avionics opens fully at 20°C and payload at 17°C. Radiator convection, sky/Earth radiation and hull radiation remain active.
  4. Handle the motor separately. Keep its original exposed surface model and raise only its heater cap to 300 W. This combined case avoids saturation without pretending that the avionics switch also insulates the motor.
  5. Check the trade-offs. Compare1%,10%,25% residual conductance, colder air, direct solar exposure, doubled RF load, battery insulation and a switch stuck at low conductance all day. Lower energy alone does not demonstrate adequate hot-case protection.

These are ideal averaged proportional heaters and continuously modulated conductances. Proportional control retains a finite temperature error: the combined motor housing still reaches about −10.6°C against its chosen −5°C target despite spare heater capacity. Physics / step by step

Follow energy through the vehicle.

Define the mission and Sun

Prescribe local solar time, relative wind and a small yaw/roll/pitch schedule. Compute the equinox Sun vector, rotate it into the body, and project it onto three roof facets and equipment surface normals. The heading comparison rotates only orientation; it holds the aerodynamic power profile fixed.

Earth IR, sky IR and reflected solar are separate terms. Effective convection uses assumed altitude coefficients with density/wind scaling. These are screening inputs, not CFD-derived heat-transfer maps or a weather forecast.

Partition electrical input correctly

Processor and telemetry input become heat. The assumed payload exports 35% as RF; the rest heats its core. Motor shaft output leaves the thermal network, while winding/controller losses remain. MPPT and DC–DC losses enter their own avionics nodes.

Used PV electrical export is subtracted from the solar laminate heat balance. Curtailed absorbed energy remains heat. Solar heating and exported electricity are not counted twice.

Resolve finite heat paths

Each thermal mass follows C dT/dt = local heat + connected heat flow. Finite contacts and straps link junction/core, mounting plate and radiator. Shared mounting structure and reciprocal hull-radiation ports pass thermal effects between subsystems.

The processor is not the tray temperature; the battery core is not its jacket; the motor winding is not the bearing/housing proxy. Twenty-four lumped nodes resolve these differences without claiming a spatial CFD field.

Combine passive rejection and active heating

Two fixed radiator paths reject waste heat; four ideal modulating heaters respond to their local sensor temperatures with finite power caps. They can saturate while the hardware continues to cool. These heater setpoints are study targets, not supplier limits.

The reference’s processor spans -12.4 to 32.4 °C and motor winding -16.2 to 38.7 °C. A lower heater budget can accompany a worse hot case or unacceptable cold exposure; energy use alone is not the design objective.

Close the battery and power bus

PV supplies DC demand and then charges the assumed 60 kWh battery, up to 12 kW with a taper near 92% SOC. Battery discharge supplies the night deficit; conversion losses heat the core. An illustrative 0–5 °C core-temperature gate affects charging.

The gate and efficiency maps require selected-cell data before design use. This is a thermal/electrical energy model, not a chemistry-specific Li–S electrochemical model or a mass/buoyancy proof.

Repeat, compare and audit

Repeat the same forcing for eight days, compare days seven and eight at matching phase, and inspect the final day. Independently reconstruct storage from component temperatures and capacities, sum boundary terms, and check electrical, thermal and thermal-plus-chemical energy.

Then refine solver step/tolerance and change one input factor per comparison. This tests numerical behavior and design sensitivity; actual properties, losses, limits and physical testing remain necessary.

Boundary and control equations inside the subsystems

ExternalSurface: hA(Tair−T) + αAGdirect + αAFearth Greflected + εA[Fearth Eearth + Fsky Esky − (Fearth+Fsky)σT⁴]. Hull IR is a separate reciprocal port: εAFhullσ(Thull⁴−T⁴). These view factors and effective coefficients are assumptions.

Heater: enabled × clamp[gain(Tset−Tsensor), 0, Pmax]. It represents averaged delivered heat; no sensor error, response lag, redundant protection or hardware actuation dynamics are solved.

Battery: dEchemical/dt = ηcharge Pcharge − Pdischarge/ηdischarge. The difference between chemical and bus power becomes heat. Efficiencies and charging permission depend on core temperature through assumed maps.

Hull/solar: thin-skin sectors and a bulk fixed-volume helium thermal node. No pressure loop, variable-volume work, buoyancy, laminate stress, internal gas circulation or detailed inner-wall radiation is solved. Optics and spatial winding/bearing/cell hot spots remain future detail.

03 / Four thermal areas, one coupled mission

Address the hardware one subsystem at a time.

Avionics

Keep junctions and sensors within their operating limits.

Resolve processor/PCB-to-case conduction, finite mounting contacts, DC–DC and distribution losses, wiring heat leaks and mode-dependent dissipation. Separate a component-average node from the hottest junction. Navigation/IMU stability can impose temperature-gradient or rate requirements.

First model: flight-computer, navigation, telemetry and power-conversion nodes tied to a conductive tray. Next evidence: loss maps, junction-to-case data, venting/pressure boundary and instrumented powered tests.

Structures and envelope

Thermal gradients also change mechanical behavior.

Evaluate solar-patch heating, shaded skin, seams, mount heat paths and the helium response over the day/night cycle. Track laminate/bondline temperatures and differential expansion. Optical or antenna mounts may need thermal-to-structural distortion mapping.

First model: separate illuminated/shaded envelope and mounting nodes. Next evidence: actual laminate properties, optical aging, gas/pressure topology, tension/strain allowables and bondline data.

Propulsion-adjacent systems

Separate motor, controller, bearing and mount losses.

The photographed topology is a stern propeller; public descriptions and patents support electric propulsion. Motor copper/iron losses, ESC switching losses, bearing limits and conduction into the mounting structure need separate paths. Air-relative speed and propwash change cooling.

First model: motor/controller/mount nodes with parameterized losses. Next evidence: electrical/shaft measurements across speed, load and pressure. Geographic station keeping does not imply zero relative airflow.

Payloads

Match the thermal design to the mission mode.

Telecom RF electronics need correct input-to-transmitted-power heat partition and duty cycles. Optical/IR payloads add detector temperature, gradient, alignment and contamination requirements. The illustrated telecom and optional optics envelopes are mission examples, not a claimed flight installation.

First model: payload electronics and mounting/aperture paths. Next evidence: the selected payload specification, dissipations, aperture boundary and thermally driven pointing limits.

Battery and solar power / shared thermal-energy constraint

Cold protection spends energy; heat rejection can increase cold exposure.

Sceye publicly identifies Li–S battery technology. That does not supply a selected cell’s heat map or charging limits. Our four module envelopes are layout placeholders. Build a chemistry-appropriate battery heat/SOC model and include heater energy in the overnight reserve. Partition solar absorption between electrical export and local heat without counting the same energy twice.

Actual cell properties, capacity, module count, pack limits, BMS logic and reserve requirements remain inputs to obtain. The public energy-density claim is not used as heat capacity or as a pack design value.

07 / From analysis to hardware evidence

Plan the measurements that could disprove the model.

Test matrix / not yet executed

Define powered ground cycles, low-pressure-air thermal balance, controlled radiative loading, battery and motor/controller loss characterization, and reviewed fault cases. Thermal-vacuum testing has different convection conditions from altitude-air operation; choose conditions matching the path being evaluated.

Instrument component hot spots, both sides of key contacts, radiator roots/tips, battery proxies, propulsion mounts and structural references. Log calibrated temperature, pressure, electrical power and heater state on a common clock. Reflective hulls complicate IR-camera temperature measurements.

Correlation / no physical data yet

Use calibration tests to fit a small observable set of uncertain parameters; predict separate holdout conditions. Compare temperature extrema, time constants, heat flow and heater energy against measurement uncertainty. Keep interface-property fitting separate from model validation.

A fit to synthetic data is a method demonstration, not an experiment. No fabricated measurements are supplied here. A future 1D/3D agreement will be a consistency check, not hardware qualification.

Cross-functional interfaces

Mechanical: CAD, contact preload, properties, venting and gradient limits. Electrical: mode losses, RF export, heater/sensor/protection logic. Systems: environment, reserves, limits and failure response. Manufacturing: bondlines, finishes, tolerances and inspection. Test: boundary reproduction, calibration and repeatability.

These responsibilities are proposed; no review with those teams has occurred in this independent study.

Standards and readiness

NASA GEVS and RTCA’s environmental-test framework are planning references. Select the actual program basis, edition, categories and tailoring before claiming compliance. The ECSS handbook endpoint was unavailable; no detailed compliance interpretation was made.

Track requirements → analysis/test cases → configuration → uncertainty → disposition. Hardware readiness needs actual build, integration, approved test and review records. The portfolio documents the approach and closes only evidence it contains.

Initial risk and design-review register

Cold battery / heater failure. Model charge/discharge limits, reserve and stuck-on/off response. Requires selected-cell data and approved hardware testing.

Payload power or contact change. Sweep losses/contact resistance and instrument both sides of the path. A cool radiator does not establish a cool junction.

Radiator solar exposure / hull shading. Trace actual orientations and view factors. A radiation sink cannot be represented by ambient air temperature alone.

Envelope/structure gradients. Couple skin/gas temperature to pressure and thermal strain when actual material/topology data exist. Geometric CAD alone supplies no structural allowable.

Motor/controller cooling loss. Evaluate load, wind and propwash conditions plus mount temperatures; do not assign all shaft/electrical power as local heat.

Unknown internals and properties. Keep sensitivity ranges and source provenance. Obtain reviewed inputs before design release or claiming predictive accuracy.
